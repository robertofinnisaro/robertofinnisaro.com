## Overview

This systematic literature review compares functional near-infrared spectroscopy
and electroencephalography as non-invasive methods for controlling upper-limb
prostheses through a Brain–Computer Interface.

The review also considers hybrid control systems that combine brain-derived
signals with surface electromyography.

## Research question

Can fNIRS provide a practical alternative to EEG for prosthetic-limb control,
particularly in real-world environments?

## Method

A two-stage literature review was conducted using studies published between
January 2017 and March 2024.

The initial database search used:

- PubMed
- Web of Science

The search combined terms relating to:

- EEG and electroencephalography
- fNIRS and functional near-infrared spectroscopy
- Robotics and prosthetics
- Amputees and hand movement

The initial search identified 604 studies:

- 213 from PubMed
- 391 from Web of Science

After duplicate removal and relevance screening, 105 studies were included in
the final review.

## EEG

Electroencephalography records electrical activity through electrodes positioned
on the scalp.

Its principal advantage is its high temporal resolution, allowing changes in
brain activity to be measured at millisecond timescales.

However, EEG signals are susceptible to:

- Electromagnetic interference
- Eye movements
- Head movements
- Muscle activity
- Environmental electrical noise

These factors can make EEG difficult to use outside controlled clinical or
laboratory environments.

## fNIRS

Functional near-infrared spectroscopy measures changes in oxygenated and
deoxygenated haemoglobin associated with brain activity.

Compared with EEG, fNIRS is less affected by electromagnetic interference and
some forms of movement-related noise.

This makes it a potentially useful modality for prosthetic systems intended for
everyday use.

Its principal limitation is its slower temporal response. The haemodynamic
response measured by fNIRS occurs over seconds rather than milliseconds, which
can introduce delays into real-time control systems.

## Hybrid control systems

Surface electromyography measures electrical activity generated by skeletal
muscles.

Combining sEMG with EEG or fNIRS may improve:

- Movement classification
- Gesture recognition
- Signal consistency
- Prosthetic control accuracy
- System reliability

However, sEMG is also susceptible to electrical noise, electrode movement,
sweating and muscle fatigue.

The review found substantially more work on EEG–sEMG systems than on
fNIRS–sEMG systems.

## Findings

The review concluded that:

1. EEG remains the most extensively researched non-invasive BCI modality.
2. EEG offers substantially better temporal resolution than fNIRS.
3. fNIRS is less susceptible to electromagnetic interference.
4. fNIRS may therefore be more suitable for some real-world applications.
5. Hybrid control systems can improve classification and control performance.
6. Research into hybrid fNIRS–sEMG prosthetic control remains limited.

## Research opportunity

A significant research gap exists around combining fNIRS with sEMG for
prosthetic control.

Future work should investigate whether muscular information from sEMG can
compensate for the slower haemodynamic response of fNIRS while retaining the
noise-resistance advantages of optical neuroimaging.

## Conclusion

fNIRS represents a promising direction for non-invasive prosthetic control.

Although its temporal resolution remains lower than EEG, its resistance to
electromagnetic interference may make it more appropriate for systems designed
to operate outside controlled laboratory environments.
